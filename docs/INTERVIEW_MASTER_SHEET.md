# Interview Master Sheet

One-sitting revision before an interview. The full Q&A bank (90 questions) is in [INTERVIEW_GUIDE.md](../INTERVIEW_GUIDE.md); this sheet is the short version.

> **Honesty line, always true:** the data is synthetic (generated by me, Superstore layout). Python, SQL and the tests are executed and verified. The Power BI report is ⟨designed and specified⟩. Say "built and validated" only after you've completed `powerbi/FINAL_VALIDATION.md`.

## 1. Project in 30 seconds
"A retailer's sales grew 6.7% in 2024 but profit fell 6.6%. I built the full analysis: Python cleaning with tests against ground truth, EDA, a MySQL star schema that matches Python to the cent, and a Power BI design with 31 DAX measures. The cause was deep discounting: every discount of 30% or more lost money overall, concentrated in the Central region and in Tables. I recommended a discount-approval pilot in Central."

## 3. Dataset
* 11,567 raw order lines → 11,461 clean; Jan 2021 – Dec 2024; 5,190 orders, 741 customers, 174 products, 4 regions.
* **Grain:** one row = one product on one order. Repeated Order IDs are normal.
* Synthetic, generated by `src/generate_data.py` (seed 42), with 11 kinds of injected errors. It uses the Superstore column layout.

## 4. Data cleaning
* **Rule:** fix before delete; delete only what can't be trusted *and* can't be rebuilt; never invent money; flag outliers, don't delete them.
* **Repaired 275 values:** Region from State (Texas → Central), names from IDs, `"$1,277.10"` → number, discount `20` → `0.20`, blanked 6 impossible ship dates.
* **Removed 106 rows:** 70 exact duplicates, 5 invalid order dates, 17 missing Sales/Profit, 14 with Sales ≤ 0 or Quantity ≤ 0.
* **Kept** 1,016 high-value outliers (42% of revenue: real copiers, phones, tables).
* **Proof:** 10 tests against the error-free original.

## 5. Feature engineering
Year/Quarter/Month/Year-Month · Shipping Days · **Gross Sales = Sales / (1 − Discount)** · **Discount Amount = Gross − Sales** · Profit Margin % (row level, for filtering only) · Discount Band · Is Loss.

## 6. EDA
* West: largest ($1.51M sales). East: best margin (13.2%). **Central: 3.3% margin.**
* 2024: sales +6.7%, profit −6.6%, margin 10.6% → 9.2%.
* South sales −12.2% a year (CAGR), but its margin is healthy (~10%).
* Tables −$91.5K and Bookcases −$30.4K: loss-making in every region.
* Margin falls from 24.9% (no discount) to 5.8% (20% discount); every level ≥ 30% is negative. Correlation −0.81.
* Seasonality: Dec, Nov and Sep are the peaks; Q4 ≈ 37% of annual sales.

## 7. SQL
MySQL 8, four scripts, executed with 0 errors. `SELECT/WHERE` → `JOIN/GROUP BY/HAVING` → `CASE` → CTEs → window functions (`LAG` for YoY, `RANK`, `DENSE_RANK`, `NTILE`, running `SUM`). PK, FK and `CHECK` constraints. Totals identical to Python: **4,391,176.00 sales, 5,190 orders**.

## 8. Star schema
`FactSales` (11,461 order lines, the numbers) + `DimDate` (1,461 days), `DimCustomer` (741), `DimProduct` (174), `DimGeography` (36). Four one-to-many relationships, single direction, dimension → fact.

## 9. Power BI
5 pages: Executive · Regional · Product · Customer · Profitability & Discount, plus a hidden Region Detail drill-through. Synced slicers: Year, Region, Category, Segment. Red only for losses. Build: `powerbi/POWER_BI_MASTER_GUIDE.md`; proof: `powerbi/FINAL_VALIDATION.md`.

## 10. DAX
31 measures. Key ones: `Total Sales = SUM(FactSales[Sales])` · `Profit Margin % = DIVIDE([Total Profit],[Total Sales])` · `Sales PY = CALCULATE([Total Sales], SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR(DimDate[Date]))` · YoY guarded with `HASONEVALUE(DimDate[Year])` · `Effective Discount % = DIVIDE([Discount Amount],[Gross Sales])` · `RANKX` with `ALLSELECTED` · a what-if `SUMX`.

## 11. Business insights
1. Growth without profit in 2024.
2. Central's low margin is **associated** with discounting: it discounts most in 15 of 17 sub-categories, and on lines discounted under 30% its margin is 17.4% vs 18.0% for the other regions.
3. Tables and Bookcases lose money everywhere. Tables lose even at 10–20% discounts, so this is a pricing problem too.
4. Deep discounts (≥ 30%) lost $249K in total.
5. South is a volume problem, not a price problem.
6. West and East deliver all of the growth and 76% of profit.

## 12. Recommendations
1. Manager approval for discounts ≥ 30%, **piloted in Central**, measuring margin *and* volume.
2. Re-price or renegotiate costs for Tables and Bookcases.
3. Put margin into sales targets.
4. Customer-retention review in the South.
5. Protect West/East capacity before the Sep–Dec peak.
**Sizing:** up to $264K, an **upper bound** (assumes unchanged volume).

## 13. Limitations
Synthetic data · only cost of goods (no shipping, returns or overheads) · associations, not proven causes · the what-if assumes the same volume · no targets or budgets · Power BI not yet built or validated in Desktop.

## 14. Difficult questions: short, correct answers

**Why synthetic data?**
To publish the project, to have realistic errors to clean, and above all to *prove* the cleaning: I kept the error-free version, so tests check every repaired value. The trade-off is that the patterns come from rules I wrote, so I present the findings as a demonstration of the method.

**Why not use a real dataset?**
Real company data is confidential. Public samples like Superstore are already clean (nothing to clean) and very common in portfolios. And with real data you can't check your cleaning against the truth. The pipeline uses the Superstore layout, so it runs on real data without code changes.

**Why Power BI?**
Managers need to filter by region, year or product themselves, without code. Power BI does that, refreshes automatically, can restrict each manager to their region (row-level security), and is the most common BI tool in analyst roles.

**Why not Tableau?**
Either would work: both build interactive dashboards on a star schema. I chose Power BI because it's very widely used in the companies I'm applying to, and because DAX measures and the Power Query/model workflow fit the KPI design well. The analysis itself (Python and SQL) is tool-independent; only the last layer would change.

**Why Python?**
For logic that must be correct and testable: cleaning rules, logging, validation, statistics. Each step is a function that a test can check against ground truth.

**Why SQL?**
Company data lives in databases, and SQL is how an analyst reaches it. It was also an independent check: MySQL matched Python to the cent. And constraints (PK/FK/CHECK) stop bad data at the source.

**Why star schema?**
Each description is stored once, filters flow predictably from dimensions to facts, time intelligence needs a proper date table, and new facts (targets, returns) can reuse the same dimensions. It's the standard BI design.

**Why not one flat table?**
At 11K rows a flat table would work. But it repeats descriptions on every row, makes "same period last year" awkward without a date table, and breaks down when a second fact table at a different grain is added (monthly targets per region). I chose the design that scales, and I can explain the trade-off.

**Why weighted margin?**
Margin = total profit ÷ total sales, so every dollar counts once. Averaging line margins gives a $3 pen the same weight as a $3,000 copier: 11.74% instead of the true 10.29% here.

**What is filter context?**
The set of filters active when a measure is calculated: slicers, page filters, and the row or column of the visual. `Total Sales` is one formula, but it shows the company total on a card, Central's total in Central's bar, and 2024 when the slicer says 2024.

**What is CALCULATE?**
It evaluates an expression after changing the filter context. `CALCULATE([Total Sales], SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR(DimDate[Date]))` keeps all other filters but shifts the dates back one year, giving last year's sales.

**Measure vs calculated column?**
A calculated column is computed once per row at refresh and stored; it doesn't react to slicers. A measure is computed at query time for the current filter context. Ratios like margin must be measures.

**How did you validate your dashboard?**
*Before building it:* every measure has an expected value computed in Python and confirmed in MySQL, listed in a checklist. *Validation:* 4 levels: row counts → relationships (functional tests) → all 31 measures → each page's visuals, recorded in a table with Expected, Actual and Pass/Fail. *(Until you've filled that table in, say "this is my validation procedure", not "I validated it".)*

**How do you know the cleaning didn't destroy information?**
70 of the 106 removed rows were exact copies, so nothing was lost there. The other 36 (0.3%) were missing or impossible values that can't be rebuilt. 275 values were repaired instead of deleted. Tests confirm no rows were invented, every repaired value equals the true value, and totals are within 1% of the error-free data.

**What if the business gives you 10 million rows?**
The design still holds; the tools scale differently. Do the heavy cleaning and aggregation in the database (SQL) or a scalable engine, not in a single pandas DataFrame. Load only the needed columns. Use incremental refresh in Power BI, and aggregation tables or DirectQuery for detail. The star schema becomes *more* important at that size, since Power BI's engine compresses narrow fact tables well. Tests run on samples plus reconciliation totals.

**What if the data contains returns?**
Returns shouldn't be deleted as "negative sales". I'd load them as their own fact table (or flagged rows) linked to the original order, then report gross sales, returns and net sales separately, and check whether some regions or products have unusual return rates. Right now, rows with Sales ≤ 0 are removed because the dataset defines returns as recorded elsewhere. With real returns data, that rule would change.

**What if management asks for forecasting?**
I'd forecast monthly sales by region with a model that handles the strong seasonality (Sep–Dec peaks): for example a seasonal baseline or exponential smoothing. I'd test it on a held-out year (train on 2021–2023, check 2024), report the error and a range rather than one number, and remember that four years of history is short. Forecasts would go into Power BI as their own table, next to actuals.

**What would you change for a real company?**
Connect to the source database instead of CSVs; re-run the quality checks (real data has new error types); add shipping, returns and targets; agree KPI definitions with Finance; interview regional managers about discount practices; run the Central pilot as a controlled test; and publish to the Power BI Service with scheduled refresh and row-level security.
